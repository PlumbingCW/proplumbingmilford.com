Old company housing and what breaks in it

Much of Hopedale was built by the Draper Corporation as a company town. The firm put up roughly 250 worker-housing buildings, and many are double houses, which means two households under one roof. The Dutcher Street homes date to 1902 through 1907, and by 1910 every house in town had water, gas, electricity and indoor plumbing.

A century-old house has had a long time to wear. Supply pipes from that era, if never replaced, can pit and weep at joints. Drain lines can scale, sag or fill with roots where they run out toward the street. Water heaters and valves added over the decades are often the newest parts, and they fail on their own schedule.

Shared walls add a twist. In a double house, a leak in a wall or a backup in a shared stack can show up on the other side first. Tell dispatch whether you own both halves or one, so the plumber knows who else to talk to.

Problems we see most often in houses this age:

  • A damp spot on a plaster wall or ceiling with no visible pipe above it.
  • Water pressure that drops at a faucet but not elsewhere in the house.
  • Slow drains that clear and return within weeks.
  • A basement floor drain that gurgles after laundry.
  • A water heater with rust at the base or a wet floor underneath.

What Hopedale homes call us for, in order

Hidden leaks come first. When a bill climbs or a meter keeps moving with every tap off, leak detection finds the pipe without opening half a wall. In plaster-and-lath houses that matters, because guessing means more repair work afterward.

Drains come second. Lines that run from a century-old house to the street are the ones most likely to hold roots or scale, so drain cleaning is a regular call. When the same clog returns, a sewer camera inspection shows whether the pipe is sagging or broken.

Water heaters come third. A tank that has served for many years and now leaks at the base cannot be patched, and the water heater replacement page explains how a swap is planned.
