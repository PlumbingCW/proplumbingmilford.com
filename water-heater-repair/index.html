Water around the heater right now

A damp floor needs a quick look before anything else, because the source decides everything. Dry the area with a towel, then watch the base and the pipes for a minute. A drip that forms on a fitting is a repair. Water that appears from under the jacket insulation means the tank is going.

We send a plumber to active leaks at any hour, and a standard repair part is often on the truck so the fix can finish on the same visit. If the part has to be ordered, the plumber can make the heater safe and tell you when the second visit is.

While you wait, protect the house:

  • Shut the cold water valve above the heater. If it will not turn, shut the main valve where the water enters the house.
  • Gas unit: set the control knob to off. A smell of gas means leave and call your gas utility or 911 first.
  • Electric unit: turn off the breaker only if you can reach the panel without standing on a wet floor.
  • Do not remove the relief valve or cap its discharge pipe. That valve is the last safety device on the tank.
  • Put a bucket under a dripping fitting and move anything stored near the unit.

Gas heaters: pilot, igniter and thermocouple

A gas heater needs three things to make hot water: gas, a flame to light it, and a sensor that proves the flame is there. If the pilot light blows out, the sensor, called a thermocouple, shuts the gas off. Relighting it works once. If it goes out again within days, the thermocouple is weak or a draft is reaching the burner.

Newer units have an electronic igniter instead of a standing pilot. A blinking status light is the unit's own error code, and the label on the tank explains it. We read the code, test the part, and replace what failed.

A heater that smells like gas or leaves soot on the burner area is a different matter. We shut it down and test the draft and venting before it runs again. Burner work on an oil unit goes to a licensed oil burner technician.

Electric heaters: elements and thermostats

Most electric tanks have two heating elements, one high and one low, each with its own thermostat. When the upper element fails you get no hot water. When the lower one fails you get a short supply of hot water that runs out fast.

The plumber checks power at the terminals, tests each element with a meter, and swaps the one that has failed. Elements are cheap compared with a tank. They still need the tank drained first, and that is why a repair visit can take a couple of hours.

A high-limit reset button on the upper thermostat trips when the water gets too hot. If it trips again after you reset it, the cause is a bad thermostat or a stuck element, not a nuisance trip.

The temperature and pressure relief valve

Every tank has a T&P valve on the side or top, with a pipe running down toward the floor. It opens if the water gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. A valve that drips constantly may have a worn seat, or it may be doing its job because pressure in the house is running high.

This is where an expansion tank often comes in. In a closed system, heating the water has nowhere to go, and the pressure spikes. We measure the pressure first, then decide whether the valve or the system needs the fix. Replacing the valve alone will not cure a pressure problem.

Never plug the relief pipe

If hot water drips from the discharge pipe, do not cap it. A blocked valve on an overheated tank is how heaters rupture. Call us.

Rumbling, popping and rusty water from sediment

Minerals and loose rust settle to the bottom of a tank over the years. The burner or element then heats the sediment layer, and trapped water flashes to steam beneath it. That is the popping you hear.

A flush clears it when caught early. A thick, stone-hard layer does not rinse out, and by then the tank is usually near the end of its service life. Rust-colored hot water only, with clear cold water, points to the tank or the anode rod rather than the house pipes.

The anode rod is a sacrificial metal bar that corrodes in place of the tank steel. Checking it every few years extends tank life. Once it is gone, the tank wall takes the attack instead.

Milford, MA: treated water and what settles out

Milford Water Company removes iron and manganese from its supply, and that treatment keeps most of those minerals out of your pipes. What it cannot tell us is how much sediment your particular tank has collected, which depends on age, usage and any private filter you added.

Hardness differs from tap to tap, so test yours. If your heater sounds like popping popcorn, ask the water company for its yearly Consumer Confidence Report, test your tap, and have the tank flushed. A flush that runs clear is a good sign, and one that runs gritty tells you what the next repair will be.
