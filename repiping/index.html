Repiping in Milford, MA

Repiping in Milford, MA means replacing the water supply pipes inside your house, not the drains, and it is a planned project rather than a rush job. Most homeowners start thinking about it after a second or third leak, or when rusty water and weak pressure become normal.

A licensed plumber looks at the pipe you have, explains the options, and gives you a clear price before work starts.

Is it time to replace the pipes?

One leak does not mean the whole house needs new pipe. A split from a freeze or a bad fitting is a repair. A pattern is different, and the pattern is what tells us repiping is worth considering.

The clearest signal is repeat failures in pipe of the same age and type. Pinholes in different rooms, green crust at many joints, and fixtures that deliver less water than they used to all point to a system wearing out from the inside.

Signs the supply pipes are reaching the end:

  • Brown or orange water that runs for a minute after a fixture has been idle.
  • Low pressure at several sinks and showers while the street pressure is fine.
  • More than one leak in the last few years, in different parts of the house.
  • Visible corrosion, flaking or rust-colored stains on exposed pipe.
  • Gray or blue plastic pipe in the basement, which may be polybutylene.

Which pipes tend to fail, and why

Age alone is a weak guide. The material and how it was installed matter more.

Pipe typeWhat goes wrongOur view
Galvanized steelZinc coating wears off, rust builds inside, the opening narrows and pressure fallsReplace once leaks or low flow appear. Patching rarely holds for long
Polybutylene (gray or blue plastic)Fails at fittings and degrades with water chemistry. Installed roughly from the late 1970s into the mid-1990sReplace. It is a known problem material
Older copperPinholes, thin walls, failing solder jointsJudge case by case. Sound copper can stay. Pitted copper should go
LeadSoft metal pipe or lead solder in very old workReplace, and ask the water supplier about records

PEX or copper: choosing the new pipe

Both materials are reliable when installed by a licensed plumber. The choice depends on your house, how much of it can be opened, and your preference. We explain the trade-offs without pushing one.

FactorPEXCopper
InstallationFlexible tubing snakes through walls with fewer cuts and jointsRigid, soldered or fitted at each turn, so more access is needed
Freeze toleranceExpands somewhat, so a freeze is less likely to split itCan split if water freezes inside

Appearance of exposed runsPlastic lookTraditional metal look

Neither material is right for every job. In a basement with open joists, copper can be a fine choice. In a finished two-story with few access points, PEX is often the practical one.

How a repiping job unfolds

We plan the work around the way you live in the house.

  1. Walk-through and map. The plumber traces the existing lines, checks the main shut-off and meter, and notes where the new runs can go.
  2. Written plan and price. You receive the scope, the pipe type and a clear price before anyone cuts anything.
  3. Permit. Replacing supply piping needs a plumbing permit pulled by a licensed plumber, and the work is inspected.
  4. Rough-in. New lines are run to each fixture, often through the same walls and chases as the old ones.
  5. Connect, test and inspect. We tie in, pressure test the system and arrange the inspection before closing up.

Phased work and living in the house

Repiping does not have to mean a week without water or a house torn open all at once. Many homes are done in phases: the basement and exposed runs first, then the kitchen and bathrooms, then the remaining branches.

Water is shut off for part of each work day, and the plumber tells you when it will be back. Phasing also lets you spread the cost, and you can pair it with other renovation work, such as a bathroom remodel covered under plumbing installation, so walls are opened once.

If you plan a full renovation, tell us before cabinets and tile go in. Running new pipe behind finished surfaces costs far more than running it first.

Drywall, access holes and patching

To reach pipe inside walls we cut small, neat access openings, typically at the top and bottom of a wall cavity or beside a fixture. In an unfinished basement, most of the work happens in open joists with no wall damage at all.

We leave openings cut square and clean so patching is straightforward. Plastering, painting and texturing are not part of plumbing work, so plan on a drywall finisher or your own painter afterward.

Milford, MA: water, permits and old houses

Milford's public water is dosed with zinc orthophosphate and a pH adjuster so it is gentler on pipe walls. That slows corrosion going forward, but it does not reverse decades of rust inside old galvanized pipe, so a house with failing galvanized needs new pipe regardless.

Plumbing permits are pulled by licensed plumbers through the town, and the work is inspected. The town posts a warning about fraudulent permit-fee emails and texts, so be careful with any message asking you to pay a fee. Questions about a specific job go to the plumbing inspector at Milford Town Hall.

Repiping is regulated work under the Massachusetts plumbing code (248 CMR), so the permit and the inspection are part of the standard process, not an extra.
