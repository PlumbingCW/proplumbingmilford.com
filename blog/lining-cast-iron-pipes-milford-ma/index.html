Lining Cast Iron Pipes: Is It Right for Your Milford Home?

Lining cast iron pipes means pulling a resin-soaked tube into the old pipe and curing it until it hardens into a new pipe inside the old one. It works when the cast iron is rough, scaled or cracking but still holds its round shape. It does not work on a collapsed or badly shifted section.

For a Milford home, the honest answer is: line it if a camera shows the pipe is intact, replace it if the pipe has failed. The camera decides, not the age of the house.

How cast iron drain pipe wears out

Cast iron is tough, which is why it stayed in houses for generations. It wears out from the inside. Wastewater is mildly corrosive, and over the decades the pipe wall flakes into rust scale and grows rough nodules. Paper, grease and wipes catch on that rough surface, and the opening gets narrower each year.

The outside can fail too. Cast iron buried under a slab or in damp soil can rust through from the outside, and a joint can leak long before the pipe itself breaks. Slow drains that return within weeks of cabling are the usual first sign.

Symptoms that point to a failing cast iron line:

  • Several drains slow down together, not just one fixture.
  • A main line clog returns soon after it is cleared.
  • A sewage odor near a floor drain or along a basement wall.
  • Rust-colored stains or flaking scale on exposed pipe.
  • Gurgling from the tub or toilet when the washer drains.

How pipe lining works, step by step

The method is often called cured-in-place pipe. The sequence is the same on most jobs, and each step matters to the result.

  1. Camera inspection. A camera runs the full length of the line first. It shows the pipe diameter, the scale, any cracks, and whether a section has dropped or separated. See our notes on sewer camera inspection for what the footage can and cannot show.
  2. Cleaning the pipe. The scale has to come out before a liner can bond. Jetting or mechanical descaling leaves a clean, rough-textured wall.
  3. Resin and liner. A flexible felt tube is soaked in epoxy resin, pulled or inverted into the pipe, then pressed against the old wall with air or water pressure.
  4. Curing. The resin hardens with hot water, steam or ultraviolet light. Curing takes hours, and the line stays out of service during that time.
  5. Reopening and a final camera pass. Branch connections are cut open from inside, and a second camera run confirms a smooth, continuous lining before the line goes back to use.

What lining cannot fix

A liner is a pipe inside a pipe. It copies the shape of whatever it is pressed against, so a belly that holds water stays a belly. A pipe that has collapsed cannot be reached by the liner tube at all.

Lining also reduces the inside diameter by a small amount. In a four-inch drain that trade is usually fine, because the new surface is smooth and flows better than the scaled iron it covers. In a very small branch line it can matter, which is another reason the camera comes first.

Active roots need to be cut out before lining, and a line with a hidden offset may call for excavation anyway. A plumber who tells you lining is the answer before seeing the footage is guessing.

Milford, MA: older pipes, the town sewer and permits

Milford was incorporated in 1780, and the town began treating wastewater in 1906, so the housing stock includes homes old enough to have original drain pipe. Houses from the early twentieth century are where cast iron under floors and in basements is most likely to be found. A camera, not a guess about the year built, tells you what your pipe actually is.

The town runs its own sewer plant and the mains under the street. The pipe from your foundation out to that main is usually the owner's. Before planning any repair near the curb, call the town Sewer Department, (508) 473-2054, and ask where that line is drawn on your street.

Under Massachusetts rules, work on sewer, drainage or vent piping is not a minor repair, so it needs a permit pulled by a licensed plumber and an inspection. Town Hall can put you in touch with the plumbing inspector about how a liner is handled. If digging is needed, call 811 before anyone breaks ground.

Lining a drain is an alteration of drain piping under the Massachusetts plumbing code (248 CMR), so the guideline is a licensed plumber, a permit and an inspection.

What affects the price of lining cast iron

Lining is priced by the job, with a clear price given before any work starts. These factors drive the number:

Length of pipeA short run under a basement floor costs far less than a line stretching from the house to the street.
DiameterLarger pipe takes more liner and more resin.
Cleaning neededHeavy scale or roots adds jetting or cutting time before the liner can go in.
AccessA cleanout at each end makes a clean job. Missing cleanouts may need to be added.
Number of branchesEach tie-in has to be reopened from inside the liner.

Most homeowner policies treat gradual pipe wear as maintenance, not a covered loss. Sudden damage may be handled differently, so read your policy before assuming a repair is covered.

Do you need a licensed plumber for this?

Yes. In Massachusetts, only a licensed master or journeyman plumber may do plumbing work, and permits for it go only to licensed plumbers. You can check any plumber's license with the Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters, and you should ask for the scope and price in writing.

Be careful with anyone who offers a quick spray-on fix without a camera. Ask for the footage before and after the lining, and ask who pulls the permit. If you live in a neighboring town, the same state rules apply, and neighbors in Hopedale and Mendon ask us the same questions.
