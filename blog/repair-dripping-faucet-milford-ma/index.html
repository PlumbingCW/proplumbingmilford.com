How to Repair a Dripping Faucet: Milford, MA Homeowner Guide

To repair a dripping faucet, shut off the water under the sink, take the handle apart and replace the worn part inside. For most faucets that part is a rubber washer or seat, an O-ring or a cartridge. The drip is a part wearing out, not a pipe problem, and a homeowner with a few hand tools can often finish in under an hour.

The steps differ by faucet type, so start by identifying yours. The sections below walk through that, the repair itself and the signs that mean you should stop and call.

Why a faucet drips in the first place

Every faucet has a part that presses against a seat to stop the flow. Over time that part hardens, cracks or gets scratched by mineral grit, and water slips past it. The drip is often at the spout, and it tends to get worse slowly.

A drip also costs money. A faucet that loses a drop every second adds up to many gallons over a month, and you pay for that water and the sewer charge on it. A small leak is cheap to fix and expensive to ignore.

Match your faucet to the likely failed part

Look at how the handle works and where the water comes from when you turn it. That tells you what is inside.

Faucet typeHow to recognize itPart that usually wears out
CompressionTwo handles that you turn several times to shut offRubber washer and brass seat
BallSingle handle over a rounded cap at the baseRubber seats and springs, sometimes the ball
CartridgeSingle or double handle that moves smoothlyO-rings or the whole cartridge
Ceramic discSingle lever, smooth and heavy feelSeals in the cylinder, rarely the discs

If you are unsure, take a photo of the faucet and bring it to a hardware counter. Match the replacement part to the brand and model, because cartridges are rarely interchangeable.

Fixing the drip, step by step

Set a towel in the sink to catch dropped screws, and keep the parts in order as they come off.

  1. Shut off the water. Turn the two small valves under the sink clockwise until they stop. Open the faucet to release pressure and confirm the flow has stopped. If a valve will not turn, stop and call.
  2. Remove the handle. Pry off the decorative cap, then undo the screw underneath. Lift the handle straight off.
  3. Pull the worn part. Unscrew the packing nut or retaining clip and take out the washer, O-ring or cartridge. Note how it sits so the new one goes in the same way.
  4. Replace and clean. Fit the new part, wipe mineral crust from the seat and rinse any grit out of the body.
  5. Reassemble and test. Put it back together, open the shutoff valves slowly and run the faucet. Watch the spout and the base for a few minutes.

When the drip is not coming from the spout

Water at the base of the faucet usually means a worn O-ring around the spout. Water under the sink is a different animal. It could be a failing supply line, a loose compression nut or a crack in the faucet body, and wet cabinet floors can hide it for weeks.

If you keep finding moisture and cannot tell where it starts, a plumber can trace it. The leak detection page describes how we find hidden leaks behind a cabinet, under a floor or inside a wall without tearing open more than needed.

Stop and call a plumber if you see any of these:

  • A shutoff valve under the sink that will not close or drips when moved.
  • Corroded or crusted fittings that crumble as you loosen them.
  • A cracked faucet body or a spout that wobbles in the sink.
  • A rotten or swollen cabinet floor under the sink.
  • A drip that returns within days of a new part.

Repair or replace the faucet

A new cartridge costs far less than a new faucet, so repair is the default. Replacement makes sense when the finish is flaking, parts are no longer made, the body is cracked or you simply want a different style.

Our faucet repair service covers both. A plumber can diagnose the leak, tell you honestly whether a part will hold and price a replacement if it will not. Parts fit differently in each brand, and a wrong part can start a new leak.

Milford, MA: water, mineral buildup and permits

Milford Water Company treats its supply with potassium hydroxide and zinc orthophosphate for corrosion control and removes iron and manganese. Hardness differs from tap to tap, so a quick test of yours is more useful than a town figure. Mineral crust on an aerator or seat is still the most common reason a repaired faucet starts dripping again, and cleaning the seat during any repair helps.

To learn what your water carries, read the utility's yearly quality report and test your own tap. A hard-water result is a good reason to descale aerators and seats each time you open the faucet.

On the rules, Massachusetts treats fixing a leaky faucet or working part of a fixture as a minor repair that needs no plumbing permit. Replacing a faucet or valve does not count as minor, so only a licensed plumber with a permit may do it. For a specific job, ask the Milford plumbing inspector through Town Hall.

Keeping the new part alive

Close handles gently. Cranking a compression faucet hard against its seat is the fastest way to wear a new washer. Clean aerators every few months, since debris trapped there raises pressure behind the faucet.

Neighbors in Mendon on private wells deal with sediment that scours seats faster, and homes in Holliston have their own water chemistry. Wherever you live, the quickest check is to look at the old washer. A pitted, gritty one tells you the water carries particles.
