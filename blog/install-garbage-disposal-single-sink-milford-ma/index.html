Install a Garbage Disposal in a Single Sink: Milford, MA

Yes, you can install a garbage disposal in a single kitchen sink, and the job is simpler than in a double sink. A single bowl has one drain opening, so the disposal bolts straight under it and its outlet connects to the trap with one short pipe. The work takes a few hours for a first-timer and involves a sink flange, a mounting ring, a drain connection and power.

Most of the risk sits in two spots: the electrical supply and the drain connection. Both are covered below, along with the point where a licensed plumber is the better choice.

What a single-sink install actually involves

A double sink has two drains joined by a tailpiece and a waste arm. A single sink skips that. The strainer in the bowl is replaced by the disposal's sink flange, and the disposal hangs from it on a three-bolt mounting assembly.

From the disposal, a short discharge pipe runs to the trap arm. If you have a dishwasher, its drain hose also connects to a small inlet on the side of the disposal. That inlet is sealed with a plastic plug from the factory, and the plug must be knocked out before the hose goes on.

Single-sink cabinets are tight. Measure the space before you buy, because a half horsepower unit is noticeably shorter than a one horsepower model.

Picking the right size for one bowl

Motor size and feed type decide how the disposal behaves day to day. This table compares the common choices for a household sink.

Tools and parts to lay out first

Gather everything before you shut the water off. A half-finished sink is a long night without a kitchen.

Have these on the counter:

  • The disposal, with its mounting kit and sink flange gasket.
  • Plumber's putty, or the gasket supplied for the flange.
  • A new trap, trap arm and discharge pipe if the old ones are corroded.
  • A power cord kit if the unit does not come with a cord.
  • A bucket, towels, slip-joint pliers, a screwdriver, and a hammer and dowel for the dishwasher plug.

Step by step: from old strainer to running disposal

Follow this order. Skipping ahead is how leaks and stripped bolts happen.

  1. Cut the power. Turn off the kitchen breaker and confirm the outlet is dead with a tester. Empty the cabinet.
  2. Remove the old strainer or disposal. Set a bucket under the trap, loosen the slip nuts, then unscrew the old flange and scrape off the putty.
  3. Seat the new sink flange. Press a rope of putty under the flange rim, or use the supplied gasket, and drop the flange into the opening.
  4. Build the mounting assembly. Slide on the gasket, backup ring and mounting ring, snap the retaining ring in, and tighten the three screws evenly.
  5. Prepare the dishwasher inlet. If a dishwasher will connect, tap out the plug in the disposal's side inlet with a hammer and dowel. Then fish the plug out of the chamber, or it will block the drain.
  6. Hang the disposal. Lift the unit onto the ring and twist until the tabs lock, with the outlet facing the trap arm.
  7. Reconnect the drain and the dishwasher hose. Refit the trap, tighten slip nuts by hand plus a quarter turn, and clamp the dishwasher hose to the inlet.
  8. Wire or plug in, then test. Plug in, restore power, run cold water and switch the unit on. Wipe every joint with a dry paper towel to catch drips.

Power: the part that decides DIY or call

A disposal needs a dedicated switch and a place to plug in, or a direct wire connection through a junction box on the unit. Many kitchens already have a switched outlet under the sink, installed years ago for a previous disposal. If yours does, plugging a cord-equipped unit into it is a simple task.

If there is no outlet, someone has to add one. Running a new circuit or wiring a switch is electrical work, and it is a separate trade. Do not improvise it. A stripped wire in a damp cabinet is a shock risk.

Milford Water Company treats its supply with zinc orthophosphate and potassium hydroxide to protect pipes from corrosion. Whatever your water, run cold water while grinding so scraps travel down the line instead of settling in the trap.

On permits, the state's consumer guidance says a plumbing permit is needed when plumbing is installed, altered, replaced or repaired, and that only licensed plumbers can pull one. Minor repairs on a fixture's working parts are exempt, but replacing a fixture or touching drain piping is not minor. When to hand it to a licensed plumber

Install it yourself if the old disposal comes out cleanly, power already exists under the sink and the trap is in good shape. Call a plumber if the drain piping is corroded, the drain arm is mis-sloped, the sink is a stone or composite model you do not want to risk, or there is no power at the cabinet.

A first-time conversion from a plain strainer is also worth a visit, since the cabinet layout may need rework. That is general plumbing installation with a clear price up front. A unit that hums, will not turn or leaks from its body belongs on the garbage disposal repair list instead.
