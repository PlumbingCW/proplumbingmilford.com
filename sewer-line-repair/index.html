Sewage is coming into the house

A sewer line that has broken or collapsed has nowhere to send the water your house produces. The wastewater rises to the lowest opening, which is usually a basement floor drain, a shower pan or a laundry standpipe. This is a health hazard and a property emergency in one.

We answer after-hours calls the same way we answer daytime ones. A plumber can come out the same night, open the line if it can be opened and show you on camera what caused the backup. The permanent repair is then planned once the camera shows what failed.

Until the plumber arrives:

  • Shut off the dishwasher and washing machine. Skip showers and flushing.
  • Keep children, pets and anyone with a weak immune system out of the affected room.
  • Do not use a drain-opening chemical. It will not fix a broken pipe and it makes the line unsafe to work on.
  • Stay out of standing water if outlets or appliances are submerged, and call 911 about the electrical hazard.
  • Do not start digging in the yard. Call 811 before any excavation, and let the plumber locate the pipe first.

Signs the problem is the pipe, not a clog

A clog is a blockage inside a healthy pipe. A failed sewer line is a damaged pipe, and the signs differ. Slow drains that return days after cleaning, wet or sunken ground over the line, and sewer gas in the basement all point toward damage.

Rodents entering the house, or a patch of unusually green grass over the line in a dry week, can also be clues. None of these proves a break, which is why the next step is a camera and not a shovel.

Common reasons a sewer line fails:

  • Tree roots entering a joint, growing into a mat and splitting the pipe.
  • Offset or separated joints where the soil has shifted.
  • Cracked or crushed clay and cast iron from age, load or frost movement.
  • A belly, which is a low spot that holds water and solids.
  • Corrosion that flakes the inside of old cast iron until the wall gives way.

How we locate the failure

Digging without a diagnosis costs money and lawn. This is the order we follow.

  1. Clear enough to see. If the line is full, the plumber opens it with a cable so the camera has something to look at. The temporary clearing is explained as temporary.
  2. Camera run. The camera travels toward the street and shows root entry, cracks, offsets, bellies or a collapse. Depth to the problem is measured with a locator.
  3. Mark the spot. The plumber marks the failure above ground, so you see where it sits relative to the house, driveway and trees.
  4. Options and price. You get the realistic repair choices in writing and in plain words, with the trade-offs, before anything is scheduled.

Spot repair, lining or replacement

The right repair depends on how much pipe is damaged and where. Here is how the three main approaches compare.

ApproachHow it worksSuitsTrade-offs
Spot repair (dig and patch)Excavate over the failure, cut out the bad section, fit new pipe with couplingsOne short damaged section in an otherwise sound lineDigs a trench, but costs less than replacing the whole run
Trenchless liningA resin-coated liner is pulled in and cured to form a new pipe inside the old oneLong runs with cracks, root-prone joints or scale, and a line that is not collapsedSmall access holes, but the pipe must be cleared first and the bore shrinks slightly
Full replacementNew pipe along the old route or a new one, often by open trench or pipe burstingCollapsed, crushed, badly offset or bellied linesMost disruption and cost, longest-lasting result

Lining cannot repair a pipe that has fully collapsed or lost a section. Replacement is the honest answer in that case, and we say so. For a line that is only clogged and not damaged, sewer line cleaning is the lower-cost route.

Roots, offsets and collapses in detail

Roots go where water and nutrients are. A sewer joint leaks a little, the roots find the moisture and slip in. Over time they thicken until they block flow and pry the joint apart. Cutting roots clears the line for a while, but the opening stays.

An offset happens when two pipe sections shift out of line. Solids catch on the lip, and paper builds up behind it. Sometimes a short spot repair fixes an offset. A bad offset at the foundation wall may need more work.

A collapse is the worst case. The pipe is crushed or has dropped out of shape, and a cable or a camera cannot pass it. Excavation is normally needed. Until the line is open again the house cannot drain normally, which is why a collapse gets emergency treatment.

Decisions homeowners ask us to explain

Many people ask whether to repair or replace. The honest answer depends on the camera. A single cracked section in a long, otherwise good line is a patch. A line with repeated cracks and root-filled joints along its length usually justifies lining or replacing the whole run, because patching one section will not stop the next failure.

Another common question is who you can trust. Only a licensed master or journeyman plumber may do this work in Massachusetts, and you can confirm any plumber's licence with the Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters at 617-727-9952. Get the scope and price in writing, ask to see the permit and confirm liability insurance.
