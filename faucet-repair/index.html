Where the water shows up tells you what failed

Faucets leak in a few predictable places, and each one has its own culprit. Water beading at the spout tip means the seat or the cartridge inside is worn. Water creeping from the base of the handle means an O-ring has hardened. A pool in the cabinet could be the faucet body, a supply line, or the drain trap.

Dry the cabinet floor, then lay a dry paper towel under each connection. The one that turns wet first is where the plumber starts.

Common signs and what they usually mean:

  • Steady drip from the spout after the handle is off: worn cartridge, washer or valve seat.
  • Handle leaks around its base: failed O-ring or loose packing nut.
  • Weak, split or sideways spray: mineral or sediment blocking the aerator.
  • Wet cabinet floor and a musty smell: slow leak at a supply line or shutoff valve.
  • Water behind the wall or through the ceiling below: stop the water at once and call.

Supply line burst or water spraying under the sink

A braided supply line that fails can pour gallons into a cabinet in minutes. Older lines corroded at the fitting fail most often, and often at night. We dispatch this as an emergency at any hour.

If the two angle stops under the sink will not turn, or they turn and the water keeps coming, go to the main shutoff, usually near the meter in the basement. Once the flow stops, the real repair is easy. The risk is the wet cabinet floor, which swells and rots quickly.

Do this while you wait:

  • Turn both angle stops under the sink clockwise. If they fail, shut the main valve.
  • Switch off power to a garbage disposal or dishwasher if the cabinet is wet and you can reach the switch dry.
  • Empty the cabinet so the floor can dry and you can see the source.
  • Do not run the faucet to test it until the plumber has looked.

When to replace the faucet instead

A repair makes sense when the body is sound and parts are easy to find. Replacement is the better call when the finish is peeling, the faucet is badly corroded inside, the spout wobbles on a cracked base, or the manufacturer no longer sells the cartridge.

If you have repaired the same faucet twice, a third repair often costs about what a new faucet does, and our plumber will say which side of that line yours is on. Replacing a faucet is not a minor repair under state guidance, so it is done by a licensed plumber and sometimes with a permit. Adding a new sink or moving a fixture falls under plumbing installation.

What the plumber checks before opening the faucet

First the water is shut off at the fixture and the line is bled so nothing sprays. Then the cartridge or washer comes out and the seat is inspected for pitting, since a pitted seat chews up a new washer in weeks.

The supply lines and angle stops get checked while the cabinet is open. A stop valve that has not been turned in years often fails to close fully, so replacing it first keeps the next repair from becoming a flood.

What to do before the plumber arrives

A faucet that is only dripping can wait, but a split hose or a failed shutoff under the sink cannot. Close the small oval valves under the sink first, turning them clockwise until the flow stops.

If the valves will not turn or leak around the stem, close the main shutoff instead and call. Never force an old valve, because a snapped stem turns a drip into a flood.

Safety steps for a leaking faucet:

  • Dry the floor and cabinet floor so nobody slips.
  • Keep power cords and strips out of the wet cabinet.
  • Do not use hot water from a faucet that sprays or spurts.
  • Place a bowl or towel under a slow drip and note how fast it fills.
