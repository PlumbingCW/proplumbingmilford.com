Signs of a hidden leak in your house

Most leaks announce themselves quietly. A stain the size of a coin on the ceiling, a musty smell in a closet, paint that bubbles on one wall, or a floor that feels soft near a bathroom all point to water you cannot see.

Sound is another clue. In a silent house, a faint hiss or the tick of a meter dial can give away a pressurized pipe that is losing water. A toilet that refills by itself every few minutes is a separate problem, but it wastes just as much and is worth ruling out first.

Common signs, in rough order of how often they turn out to be a real leak:

  • Your water bill rises with no change in how many people live in the house.
  • The meter dial moves while every fixture and appliance is off.
  • A ceiling or wall stain grows after a shower or after the dishwasher runs.
  • Water pressure drops at several fixtures at once.
  • Part of the lawn stays wet or sinks while the rest dries out.
  • A hot spot appears on a floor over a concrete slab.

Water is spreading right now

A leak becomes an emergency when water is actively soaking drywall, dripping through a ceiling, or collecting in a basement faster than you can mop. We dispatch these at any hour, Christmas night included, and the plumber starts by stopping the water, then finds the source.

The first ten minutes decide how much of the house gets wet. Know where your main shut-off valve is before you need it. In most homes it sits near where the water line enters the basement, often next to the meter.

Until the plumber pulls up:

  • Turn the main shut-off clockwise until it stops. A ball valve with a lever closes with a quarter turn.
  • Open one faucet at the lowest point to drain the pressure from the line.
  • Keep clear of wet outlets, power strips and anything plugged in near the water.
  • If water is dripping around a light fixture, do not touch the switch. Shut off that circuit at the panel only if the floor around the panel is dry.
  • Catch drips with buckets and lay towels along the edge of a growing stain.

The two-minute water meter test

The meter is the most honest witness in the house. This test tells you whether water is leaving the pipes when nothing should be using it.

  1. Shut everything off. Turn off every faucet, make sure the dishwasher and washer are idle, and pause the ice maker if it is hooked up.
  2. Read the dial. Find the small triangle or star on the meter face. If it spins, even slowly, water is moving.
  3. Note the numbers. Write down the full reading, wait twenty minutes with nothing running, and read it again.
  4. Close the house valve and repeat. If the dial stops once the main valve inside the house is closed, the leak is in the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the supply line between the meter and the house.

That last check saves hours. It tells us whether to start in the walls or out in the yard, and it is the first question dispatch will ask.

Wet ceilings and walls

A ceiling stain rarely sits directly under its source. Water runs along joists and pipe wraps before it surfaces, so the damp patch can be several feet from the actual drip. Upstairs bathrooms, a tub overflow gasket, a shower valve and a supply line to a second-floor sink are the usual suspects.

Our plumbers read moisture first. A thermal camera shows the cool, wet area behind drywall without opening it, and a moisture meter confirms how far the dampness reaches. Then we run a pressure test on the suspect line to confirm that the pipe, not the roof or an air conditioner drain, is the source.

If the leak turns out to be a failed supply line, the fix is a short section of new pipe, and we cut only the drywall needed for access.

Basement and crawlspace leaks

Below the house, water gives itself away differently. Drips from a joint, a copper pipe with green crust at a fitting, or a puddle that returns after you wipe it up all indicate a supply or drain problem. A damp floor with no pipe above it is more likely groundwater, which is a different job.

That distinction matters. If the water appears along the wall after rain, the cause is outside the plumbing. The basement waterproofing page covers that situation. If the puddle grows on dry days, a pipe is losing water, and a crawlspace inspection by flashlight and thermal camera usually shows which one.

Check the sump pit, too. A pump that cycles constantly on a dry day suggests water is entering from somewhere, and the sump pump repair page explains what to look at.

Slab leaks: warm spots, sound and a running meter

A slab leak is a break in a pipe that runs under or inside a concrete floor. It is less common in New England, where most homes sit on basements, but it does occur in additions, garages and slab-on-grade ranches.

The signs are specific. A warm patch on the floor suggests a hot water line is leaking beneath it. A meter that runs with all fixtures off is a strong indicator. Faint running-water sound from the floor, a damp carpet edge, and sudden rises in the water bill complete the picture.

We pinpoint the break with acoustic equipment and a thermal scan, then discuss options: cutting the slab directly over the leak, or rerouting the line through the walls and ceiling. A second leak in the same pipe is a reason to look at the repiping page before another patch.

Underground supply line leaks

The pipe between the street and your house is buried, so a leak there can run for weeks without a stain on any ceiling. The clues are a wet patch in the yard on a dry week, a sunken stretch of driveway, a meter that spins when the house valve is shut, or pressure that drops and recovers without explanation.

We confirm the leak with a pressure test and listening gear, then mark the likely location before any digging. Repair or replacement of that pipe is covered on our water service line repair page, including who owns which section.

Irrigation and pool lines

Irrigation systems leak at valves, heads and the underground pipe between zones. A zone that stays soggy, a head that sprays weakly, or a bill that jumps in summer points to the system. Shutting the irrigation supply and watching the meter separates that from a house leak.

A short word on pools. Pool plumbing runs underground and under pressure from the pump, so leaks show up as a falling water level that does not match evaporation. We can pressure test the supply and return lines. Pool structure and liner repairs belong to a pool contractor.

Milford, MA: your meter and your pipes

Milford Water Company supplies the town and doses the supply with zinc orthophosphate and a pH adjuster to hold down corrosion. That treatment is aimed at protecting pipes, but older metal plumbing still wears from the inside over decades, and pinhole leaks in aging copper or galvanized lines are a practical result.

Because the meter belongs to the water supplier, ask Milford Water Company about your account if a reading looks wrong. We can tell you whether the pipes on your side of the meter are the cause, which is often the first thing to settle on a high bill.

Finding a leak is not a repair, but fixing what you find usually is, and under the Massachusetts plumbing code, the standard in this state, repairs to supply or drain piping need a licensed plumber and often a permit.
