What hydro jetting does inside a pipe

A jetting machine pumps water through a hose to a nozzle with several small orifices. Most of the jets point backward, which pulls the hose forward along the pipe. A few point forward or sideways to break up what is ahead. The water scrubs the whole inner wall in a full circle, not just the center.

That is the main difference from a cable. A cable spins a blade through the middle of the blockage and leaves a channel. Grease and scale stay stuck to the pipe wall, and the line slowly narrows again. Jetting removes the coating itself, so the pipe returns to something close to its original bore.

Signs a line needs more than a snake

A single clog is a nuisance. A pattern is a diagnosis. If the same drain has been cleared two or three times in a year, the pipe wall is probably coated, and clearing the middle again will not hold.

These patterns often point toward jetting:

  • A kitchen line that clogs again within weeks of being snaked.
  • A main line that drains slowly after every laundry day.
  • Heavy home frying or batch cooking that sends a lot of fat down the sink.
  • A floor drain that gurgles when a toilet flushes upstairs.
  • Rough, flaking rust on the cable when it comes back out of old cast iron.

Sewage backing up right now

Wastewater rising from a floor drain, a tub that fills when the washer drains, or a toilet that will not clear is an emergency at any hour. A fully blocked main can push wastewater into a finished basement within minutes of the next shower.

Call and tell dispatch which drains are affected. Nights and weekends are handled as same-day calls, and the first job is to stop the backup. Jetting may follow once the plumber has looked inside the pipe.

While you wait:

  • Stop the washer, dishwasher and showers. Every gallon has nowhere to go.
  • Keep children and pets out of the wet area and wear boots if you must enter.
  • Leave chemical drain opener on the shelf. It can splash back at the plumber.
  • If water is touching outlets, appliances or the panel, stay clear and call 911 about the electrical hazard first.

When jetting is the wrong tool

Water at jetting pressure is gentle on healthy pipe and unkind to a damaged one. In a cracked clay line, an offset joint or a section of thin-walled cast iron, a strong jet can widen the break or push debris and water into the soil around it.

Old cast iron is a common case. Decades of corrosion can leave the wall thin under its scale, and the scale may be the only thing holding it together. We would rather cable gently, camera the line and recommend a repair than blast away the last of the pipe.

Jetting cannot fix a collapsed pipe, a belly holding standing water, or a missing section. Those need the repairs described on the sewer line repair page.

How the visit runs, start to finish

We look before we blast. The order matters more than the machine.

  1. Camera pass first. A small camera on a flexible rod goes in through a cleanout or a pulled toilet. It shows grease, roots, scale, offsets and breaks, and it tells the plumber whether jetting is safe.
  2. Price and plan. You hear what was found, which method is recommended and the price before the jetting machine is set up.
  3. Jet the line. The nozzle is fed in slowly, working forward and then pulling back to scrub the walls. Debris is flushed toward the main and the town sewer.
  4. Camera again. A second pass confirms the walls are clear and the pipe is intact. You can see the result, not just hear it.
  5. Next steps. If roots keep returning at a joint or the pipe is damaged, you get the options in plain words with no pressure to decide that day.

Grease, scale and roots: what jetting handles

Grease is the easy win. Hot cooking fat cools in a drain, hardens into a waxy layer and collects starch and food scraps. Jetting cuts that layer away and flushes it out, which a cable seldom manages.

Scale is mineral and rust buildup on the inside of older iron and steel. It narrows the pipe and gives other debris something to catch on. A jet can strip loose scale, but it cannot restore a pipe that has corroded through.

Roots are more mixed. A jet with the right nozzle can cut fine root hair and mats that a cable leaves behind. A thick root that has forced its way into a joint will grow back, because the gap is still there. In that case jetting buys time and the joint needs a fix.

Milford sewer lines and what you can jet

Milford homes on town sewer send wastewater to the Town of Milford's treatment plant, which has been treating wastewater since 1906 and discharges to the Charles River. Homes of different ages tie into it, and older houses may still have original iron or clay drain pipe.

Age matters for jetting. A pipe laid decades ago is more likely to be scaled, rooted or fragile, which is why we run the camera before choosing the nozzle. If a camera shows your house is fine and the street main is the problem, the Milford Sewer Department at (508) 473-2054 handles the town side.

The Massachusetts plumbing code (248 CMR) is the standard for drain work, and a licensed plumber should confirm the pipe can take the pressure before jetting begins.
